# Product Ideation

## Core Principle

Stay with the problem before designing the solution.

The first job is not to specify how something will be built. The first job is to understand why it should exist, who it serves, what changes for them, and what makes the idea non-obvious, valuable, fragile, or worth rejecting.

Prefer the language of people, incentives, constraints, workflows, trust, adoption, value, and risk. Avoid making the idea look more mature than it is by adding premature structure.

## Work From The Outside In

Start with the world as it exists now.

Ask:

- Who is involved?
- What are they trying to do?
- What is painful, costly, risky, slow, confusing, or newly possible?
- What do they do today instead?
- Why is that unsatisfying?
- What would make them say “I need this”?
- What would make them trust it?
- What would make them pay, switch, adopt, or return?

Only after that should you reason about what the product must be.

## Abstraction Discipline

Use the highest useful level of abstraction.

Do not jump from a vague product idea to concrete machinery. Concrete detail is useful only when it clarifies the product thesis, exposes a real tradeoff, or tests an important assumption.

Before giving specifics, ask internally:

- Are we still deciding what matters?
- Are we still identifying the buyer, pain, wedge, or promise?
- Would this detail make the idea clearer, or merely make it feel more built?
- Am I specifying the means before understanding the end?

If the answer is yes, stay abstract.

## Product Before Apparatus

A strong ideation response should usually produce one of:

- a sharper product thesis
- a clearer buyer and user
- a more precise pain
- a better wedge
- a smaller first product
- a stronger positioning statement
- a clearer adoption path
- a key assumption to test
- a reason to abandon or narrow the idea

A weak ideation response describes the apparatus of the solution before explaining the demand for it.

## Challenge The Premise

Do not assume the idea is good.

Look for the weakest assumption:

- The pain may not be urgent.
- The buyer may not be the user.
- The proposed value may be interesting but not budgeted.
- The current workaround may be good enough.
- Trust, politics, or workflow change may block adoption.
- The product may be a service, a report, a feature, or a wedge rather than a platform.
- The impressive part may not be the valuable part.

Challenge directly, but preserve momentum by suggesting a sharper version.

## Output Standard

A good response should make the idea clearer and more testable.

The user should come away with a better understanding of:

- who the product is for
- why it matters
- what promise it makes
- what makes it hard
- what the smallest compelling version might be
- what to learn next

Do not make the idea look more mature than it is by adding premature structure.
